### 1) Tiered AI rigor: cheap models find 75% of bugs at 4% of the cost — but they fail hardest where mistakes are most expensive

- Track: better_decisions
- Why it matters to me: Entelligence benchmarked GPT-5.6 Luna ($0.20/$1.20 per M tokens) against GPT-6 Astra ($10/$50) on the same 50 PRs: Luna verified 69 of 143 bugs at 74% precision for $0.20 total; Astra verified 92 at 96% precision for $5.66. Cost per verified bug: $0.003 vs $0.061 (~20x). The gap is concentrated — Luna found only 6 of 14 verified bugs in Keycloak (identity/permissions) and 9 of 24 security bugs; it was near-tie on data/logic bugs. It also uniquely caught 25 bugs Astra missed (mostly data/logic). A reusable decision rule: **route AI effort by risk class, not by uniform budget** — cheap-and-retryable work goes to the $0.003 tier; irreversible, security-critical, permission-adjacent work goes to the frontier tier.
- Tradeoff / tension: A ~25% false-positive rate (Luna: 24 of 93 findings unverified) is the hidden cost. Engineers who already skim AI review will skim harder when a quarter of it is noise — precision erosion destroys the trust that makes the tier cheap in the first place. Uniform "best model everywhere" overspends 20x; uniform "cheapest everywhere" silently fails on the code class where one missed bug is an incident.
- Implication: Every recurring AI task you have (or your team has) can be re-priced with this framework: (1) classify the task by irreversibility and blast radius, (2) assign a model tier per class, (3) measure acceptance/verification rate as the trust metric, not cost. Same logic applies to review of AI-written output, not just AI-generated code.
- Action: Act — pick one recurring workflow (e.g., code review or doc review) and split it into "retryable" vs "irreversible" tasks; route the retryable ones to the cheapest competent model and spot-check the irreversibles. Reuse this classification in the knowledge base as a reusable model: "risk-tiered model routing."
- Confidence: medium (single vendor's benchmark, published by a company that sells the expensive tier; methodology is transparent and replicable, but the 20x claim benefits the vendor)
- Source: Entelligence — https://entelligence.ai/blogs/gpt-5.6-luna-vs-gpt-6-astra-is-a-1.20-model-good-enough-for-code-review

### 2) Amazon vs. Perplexity: the 9th Circuit draws the agent-liability line at the human user

- Track: ai_change
- Why it matters to me: Amazon sued Perplexity under the CFAA, claiming its Comet browser's "Assistant" (which navigates Amazon on the user's behalf and screenshots pages to Perplexity's servers) was unauthorized access. The 9th Circuit vacated the preliminary injunction on a key holding: the access was performed **by the user employing the assistant as a tool**, not by Perplexity — so Amazon was unlikely to succeed on the merits. This is one of the first circuit-level statements on where legal responsibility for agent behavior sits.
- Tradeoff / tension: For agent builders, the holding frees up agent browsing but moves the entire liability surface to the end user — meaning "I asked the assistant to" becomes the legal defense and platforms lose CFAA leverage against agents as products. For you as a future agent operator: your agent's footprint is *your* footprint. Also note the stage — this is an appeal of a preliminary injunction, remanded, not a final merits ruling; the doctrine can still move.
- Implication: Watch the CFAA-agent-access doctrine as a leading indicator. If agent access keeps landing on the user side, platforms will defend with technical blocks (rate limits, ToS, bot detection) rather than CFAA suits — which favors well-designed agents and hurts casual scraping. For any business of yours that uses agents to touch third-party systems, the risk model just shifted from "vendor liability" to "your user's liability."
- Action: Watch — add "agent access & CFAA doctrine" to the knowledge base as a concept page with this ruling as anchor; revisit when the remand decision lands.
- Confidence: medium (real appellate holding, but preliminary-injunction stage, single circuit, likely to be re-litigated)
- Source: 9th Circuit (Amazon v. Perplexity, No. 26-1444) via Justia — https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/26-1444/26-1444-2026-08-04.html

### 3) Fyxer: 500,000 hours of human EA work as the moat, decomposed into 30–50 tiny models

- Track: own_business
- Why it matters to me: Fyxer's AI executive assistant is built on years of running a *human* EA service first — 500K+ hours of annotated real workflows — then decomposed the email problem into 30–50 specialized small models (reply-needed classifier, intent predictor, tone-matched drafter, retrieval/memory re-ranker) instead of one big generation task. Reported: 90% retention at 90 days, 53% of AI drafts accepted as written. Two operator patterns here: (1) **service-first, product-second** — doing the labor yourself is dataset production, not just revenue; (2) **decomposition beats one-shot prompting** on any subjective, context-heavy task.
- Tradeoff / tension: The moat (labeled operational data) is only valuable if the workflow is repeatable, personal, and trust-gated — and the 53% acceptance rate says "as written" still fails nearly half the time. The cost side: maintaining dozens of models is far more operational complexity than calling one frontier API. This pattern also warns that "the model got better" is not a moat — it's the data + the narrow task decomposition + the trust loop (user feedback retraining) that is.
- Implication: For your own business: if you're choosing between two wedges, score them on "can I do the manual version of this job now and label it along the way?" Wedges where you can *be* the assistant today are ones where the dataset compounds. Also apply decomposition to anything you build on LLMs: if a single prompt task feels hard, it's probably 10 small prediction problems.
- Action: Think — write a one-pager: "the jobs I (or my users) do by hand where my performance data would train the product." If the list has fewer than two entries, that's information about your wedge selection.
- Confidence: high (vendor case study — numbers are self-reported and cherry-pickable, but the architectural lessons are consistent with what the top agent products all converge on)
