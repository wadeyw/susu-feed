### 2) There's No Limit to How Bad Code Can Get

- Track: better_decisions
- Why it matters to me: Zach Kehs's insight — "If you continue to add floors and rooms to a building forever, it will collapse. Software faces no such constraint." — is a mental model upgrade for how I think about technical debt, rewrites, and engineering decision-making. Simon's follow-up comment reinforces it: announcing that the old system is "irrecoverably drowning" and then spinning up a rewrite team almost never works, because the old system keeps moving while you build the new one. This directly serves AQ4 about failure modes and decision hygiene.
- Tradeoff / tension: The tension is between the seductive clarity of a clean-slate rewrite and the messy reality of incremental improvement. A rewrite promises a clean architecture, modern tooling, and removed legacy baggage — but it requires maintaining two systems simultaneously, risks losing tribal knowledge, and often ships to find that the "old problems" were actually business problems, not code problems. The counter-tradeoff: never rewriting means accumulating debt that eventually makes any change prohibitively expensive. The real decision isn't "rewrite or not" — it's "what is the marginal cost of improvement in the current system vs. the replacement system, and when does that crossover happen?"
- Implication: Treat "let's rewrite it" as a hypothesis that requires extraordinary evidence, not a default instinct. Before committing to a rewrite, ask: (1) What specific problems does the current system prevent us from solving that a new system would enable? (2) What tribal knowledge and implicit contracts live in the old code that we'd lose? (3) Are we solving a code problem or a business problem? Most rewrite decisions fail because they misdiagnose the constraint.
- Action: When you encounter a system you want to rewrite, write down the three questions above before the conversation escalates. If you can't answer them with specificity, the instinct to rewrite is likely emotional, not analytical. Consider whether targeted refactoring of the highest-leverage components would achieve 80% of the benefit at 20% of the cost.
- Confidence: high
- Source: Simon Willison — https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/6/theres-no-limit-to-how-bad-code-can-get/
