### 2) Bryan Cantrill: "The Contagion of Fear" — a claims filter for the AI discourse
- Track: better_decisions
- Why it matters to me: A rare essay that gives an operational epistemics framework for a topic I'm constantly fielding (extinction-risk claims went mainstream this week: ex-Anthropic researchers claiming ">10% chance AI kills all humans within a decade"). Three durable principles: (1) the public *must* trust domain experts, so experts hold an implicit trust debt — the burden of proof on extraordinary claims sits with the claimant, not the audience; (2) "the sheer number of frightened experts becomes its own kind of evidence" — apparent consensus by din, drowning out contrary voices. Headcount is not a probability; (3) "Intelligence is not Enough": acts of engineering require action in the physical world, and AI agency is agency *we permit* through systems we built. A time horizon inconsistent with physical reality is a red flag, not a mystery to solve in the claimant's favor.
- Tradeoff / tension: Cantrill's own vulnerability: he conflates "this fear was sown irresponsibly" (epistemics) with "the risk is therefore low" (the estimate). His rebuttal is strongest on the claim-filter and weakest on the probability question — a genuine catastrophic tail is not disproven by a contagion argument. Use it as a filter on claims, not as a risk estimate. Also note his move toward dismissiveness could backfire if a real signal arrives in the same clothing.
- Implication: A three-line filter for any high-stakes AI claim (including my own beliefs, and the ones I communicate to my family): who bears the burden of proof, is the evidence commensurate with the claim, and which physical-world step actually has to happen? For me personally: as a domain expert I hold implicit trust with my team and my kids — I should not exaggerate in either direction. The Cantrill line that stung me: "if you have a newborn… greater than ten percent chance your child perishes at the hands of AI before middle school" — that's the exact anxiety level I don't want to import into the household; the filter is how to keep it out.
- Action: think — apply the three-line filter to one claim I'm currently carrying (e.g., "my skill set will be obsolete by year X"): write down what evidence actually supports it and which physical step is required. One page, ten minutes.
- Confidence: medium (thoughtful opinion from a credible operator; a claims-filter, not a measurement — and I've flagged where his argument leaks)

### 3) Running routes via one-line agent request: the build-vs-delegate line is moving
- Track: ai_change
- Why it matters to me: Small but concrete capability update: a 27-minute general-agent run (GPT-6 Astra via ChatGPT Work) took a one-line request ("figure out 5K/10K loop routes from my house, use OSM data") and produced embedded visualizations plus downloadable GPX and GeoJSON — via Nominatim → Overpass → OSM, with the agent able to explain its own tool trace when asked. The reusable principle: one-off real-world data tasks are increasingly delegable to a general agent instead of "build a small app." Simon's self-explanation step (ask "how did you do that?" → get the tool trace) is part of the pattern — auditability on demand.
- Tradeoff / tension: One-off agent output is a black box per run; the cost of delegation is reproducibility and audit. The self-trace helps but doesn't replace verification. The natural boundary: one-off + local data + no irreversible consequence → delegate; recurring + shared + irreversible (money, code, external submissions) → build. Where the failure rate becomes unacceptable is the open question — 27 minutes for a 5K route is impressive, but what does the same setup do with a financial analysis or a legal document?
- Implication: A personal "delegate-vs-build" decision rule to test against my actual recurring chores (research syntheses, data pulls, report scaffolds). This also quietly de-risks the "every tool I might build" list — several of my candidate one-off utilities now default to "just ask."
- Action: act (small) — this week, hand one real recurring one-off task (a research synthesis into a structured doc, or a data pull I currently do by hand) to a general agent, time it, and record whether the output was trustworthy without checking. That one data point calibrates my build-vs-delegate line better than any forecast.
