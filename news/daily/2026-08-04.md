### 2) Don't be a meat proxy
- Track: daughters_future
- Why it matters to me: "Meat proxy" is the new term for a real pattern — people who use AI to generate output and then transmit it as their own without genuine understanding. This is exactly the skill failure mode to prepare against. If the daughters grow up in a world where AI-generated content is ubiquitous, the valuable human signal becomes: did you actually think this through?
- Tradeoff / tension: Using AI to draft, research, and ideate is legitimate. The line is crossed when you transmit output without reading, understanding, and rewriting it in your own voice. The tension is practical: AI makes it easier to produce, which makes it harder to verify you actually understand what you're producing. Verification takes time that AI saved.
- Implication: Teaching the habit of "read it, understand it, rewrite it" is now a core literacy skill. The ability to validate AI output — to spot when it's wrong, shallow, or misaligned — is the new form of expertise. This connects directly to the "LLMs reward expertise" signal: you can't be a good meat-proxy-rejector without genuine understanding.

### 3) The Dunning-Kruger effect may just be a data artefact
- Track: better_decisions
- Why it matters to me: This is a widely-used mental model about expertise and confidence that may not actually exist as described. If the statistical effect is an artifact of regression to the mean, then the narrative we've built around "incompetent people overestimate themselves" needs revision. This matters for how we teach judgment, self-assessment, and when to trust (or distrust) confidence.
- Tradeoff / tension: The D-K effect is useful as a heuristic even if it's not a robust statistical phenomenon. Dismantling it risks losing a practical warning against overconfidence. But keeping it risks building decision frameworks on a false foundation. The tradeoff is between practical utility and truth — and in the long run, false models corrode judgment.
- Implication: If D-K is an artifact, we need a better model for the relationship between competence and confidence. Possible candidates: calibration training, explicit uncertainty estimation, and feedback loops that correct overconfidence. For the daughters_future track, this means teaching explicit calibration (calibrating confidence to actual accuracy) rather than relying on the vague "don't overestimate yourself" narrative.

- Confidence: medium
- Source: McGill University OSS — https://www.mcgill.ca/oss/article/critical-thinking/dunning-kruger-effect-probably-not-real

### 4) Devtools must be open source
- Track: ai_change
- Why it matters to me: Simon's argument is structural, not sentimental: LLMs have changed the cost of understanding software. Before, reading and modifying code required years of investment that most people couldn't justify. Now, an LLM can help a competent adult read and modify code they don't fully understand. This shifts the economics of software ownership and maintenance.
- Tradeoff / tension: Open source gives you the freedom to lean on others (including AI) to understand and modify your tools. Proprietary software still works — but the gap between "can use" and "can understand/fix" widens as AI lowers the barrier to the former without lowering it for the latter. The tension is between convenience and resilience: proprietary tools may work better today, but open source gives you the option to survive tomorrow when the vendor changes terms, raises prices, or disappears.
- Implication: For own_business, this means tooling choices have long-term optionality implications. For ai_change, this is a structural shift in who can build vs. who can only consume. The "openness" of devtools becomes a hedge against vendor lock-in in an era where AI makes understanding code cheaper for everyone — except when the code is hidden.
