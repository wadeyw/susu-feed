### 1) Stripe's fraud data: AI startups carry a structurally larger abuse surface — and the center of gravity is moving upstream

- Track: own_business
- Why it matters to me: Stripe's network data (billions of transactions) shows AI startups hit a 4.3x higher attempted transaction-fraud rate than startups overall in Q3 2025, down to 2.6x by Q1 2026 as tactics rotated. Meanwhile attempted multi-account abuse across AI subscription companies rose 40% in six months (Jan–Jun 2026), with the most-abused companies up 154% and some over 600% (ElevenLabs blocked ~2,000 abusers/day). The mechanism is AI-specific: compute and tokens are *resellable* — a stolen card buys a subscription or token block, the actor resells the access (or on secondary markets or in regions where the product isn't available), and the legitimate cardholder disputes by the time resale is complete. The fraud surface you can't price as a normal SaaS COGS line.
- Tradeoff / tension: Controls work but they trade friction for abuse. Heavy signup verification (device checks, prepay, limits) suppresses free-tier farming but depresses activation and conversion — the two metrics most early AI products live on. The data shows fraud doesn't disappear, it *shifts*: as transaction fraud got harder (4.3x → 2.6x), actors moved to multi-account abuse and free-tier farming. So "we fixed fraud" is never true; it's "fraud moved to the next lifecycle stage." Expect a permanent cat-and-mouse across signup → payment → usage, not a one-time fix.
- Implication: If any path of yours involves a self-serve AI product, bake an **expected-abuse line** into unit economics: expected_abuse ≈ price × resellability of what you sell × gap in signup verification. AI products need to carry it; most SaaS competitors don't — which is a real cost advantage of non-AI wedges that's rarely priced. Also: signup-stage risk assessment is now product work, not billing work.
- Action: Think — write the expected-abuse formula into the knowledge base as a concept ("AI products have structurally larger fraud surfaces because the output is resellable compute"); revisit it the next time a self-serve AI wedge is on the table, and check whether the idea being considered has resellable output.
- Confidence: medium-high (Stripe's own cross-network data, methodically stated; caveat: "attempted" rates, not confirmed losses, and Stripe sells the tool that fixes it — incentive skew)
- Source: Stripe Blog — https://stripe.com/blog/what-stripe-data-shows-about-fraud-at-ai-startups

### 2) Willison's "influences" post: what a durable knowledge system actually holds

- Track: better_decisions
- Why it matters to me: In a Lobsters thread on which blog posts shaped his thinking most, Willison names exactly three: Spolsky's *Law of Leaky Abstractions* (kept him "always looking for the layers under where I'm working"), Lethain's *Migrations* (treat replacing services/engines as an ordinary, repeatable skill — "the sole scalable fix to tech debt" — not a heroic one-off), and Charity Majors' *Engineer/Manager Pendulum* (pendulum between IC and management multiple times; it makes you better at both). The shared property is what matters: each is a **small, durable model, re-applied over 15–20 years, still generating value today**. This is an empirical argument for what belongs in an external knowledge base: a handful of load-bearing frameworks, not a growing pile of notes.
- Tradeoff / tension: Durable models are compressive — each one will mislead you in edge cases, and the cost of "few, deeply internalized" is occasionally the wrong tool at a moment when the nuance mattered. The alternative (many notes, broad retrieval) has its own failure mode: notes surface easily but rarely *transfer*, because nothing is internalized. There's no free lunch: a small framework core maximizes re-application, at the cost of coverage.
- Implication: Directly applicable to the Supabase wiki + skills architecture you're building: the top-level pages should be the 5–7 frameworks you've actually re-applied in the last five years, each with (a) the model in one paragraph, (b) when it applies, (c) when it breaks, (d) evidence of times it paid off. Notes and summaries hang *under* frameworks, not the other way around. Retrieval then becomes "which framework is this situation an instance of?" — which is a better question than "what did I read about X?" Also worth stealing Lethain's move for any team you lead: migration/replatforming should be an explicitly trained, routine capability, not an emergency.
- Action: Act (30 min) — draft your own top-5 framework list (one paragraph each: model, applies-when, breaks-when, payoff-evidence). Seed them as knowledge-base top-level pages. Audit each one quarterly: "did this framework generate new value this quarter?"
- Confidence: high (the argument is verifiable — three named sources, each a canonical essay; the design implication is your move)
- Source: Simon Willison — https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/14/influences/

### 3) Gemini Live: the voice-agent layer is now a two-vendor commodity, and the UI build path collapsed further

- Track: ai_change
- Why it matters to me: Google shipped Gemini 3.8 Live and 3.8 Live Extended Thinking — two speech-to-speech models "a similar shape to OpenAI's GPT-Live models." Real-time voice is now a two-vendor category, which is the moment a layer becomes commodity. The operator-grade detail: Willison didn't hand-build the test UI — he pointed GPT-6 Astra at Google's docs, one-line request, got a working web UI with model/voice selection, system prompt, and mid-speech interruption. This is now the *third* instance in ten days of one-line agent requests shipping real interfaces (Blender, running routes, this one).
- Tradeoff / tension: For anyone planning voice-first surfaces: the model + UI layer is cheap and commoditizing, so differentiation moves to what's left — domain integration, latency, and interruption/thinking UX. The "Extended Thinking" variant is the new design axis: voice channels now have to choose *speed vs. depth* deliberately, because a voice answer that thinks too long dies the conversation, and one that doesn't think is shallow. There's no free default.
- Implication: The question on any user-facing project shifts from "can we afford a voice surface?" (no — it's now a one-line build + commodity model) to "does voice add value for *this* user, and can we tune interruption behavior acceptably?" Build cost is no longer the constraint; product judgment is.
- Action: Watch — optionally, spend 30 minutes on a small personal use case (e.g., a voice interface to your knowledge-base retrieval) to build felt intuition on interruption UX; the build is a one-line agent request now, so the experiment is cheap.
- Confidence: medium (two-vendor observation is factual; the "commodity" call is extrapolation — Google/OpenAI could still differentiate on quality for a while)
- Source: Simon Willison — https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/15/gemini-live/
