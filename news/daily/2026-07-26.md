### 1) Stanford SIEPR: What's really happening to jobs — separating AI hype from reality
- Track: ai_change
- Why it matters to me: This is one of the first serious empirical syntheses on AI's actual labor market impact, and it directly challenges the dominant narrative. The data shows aggregate employment effects are small so far, but there's a real canary-in-the-coalmine effect for young workers in entry-level roles. This updates the mental model from "AI will displace white-collar workers massively" to "AI is reshaping hiring patterns, especially at the bottom, while firm-level adoption correlates with employment growth."
- Tradeoff / tension: AI adoption at the firm level correlates with 10% employment growth over two years — but this masks a distributional shift: seniors grow, juniors shrink. The productivity gains are real (15% overall, 30% for novices in customer support), yet creative diversity may decline as AI-homogenized output replaces human exploration.
- Implication: The labor market disruption from AI is not a mass layoff event but a hiring-freeze-and-consolidation event. For someone building a business, this means junior talent is cheaper and more available now but may be harder to develop internally. The PC analogy holds: early adoption accelerates before aggregate benefits appear, and the 1980s saw rapid PC adoption with delayed productivity gains.
- Action: Watch whether your own hiring patterns shift toward senior-only as AI tools mature. Consider this an opportunity to hire experienced people who can leverage AI without needing heavy mentorship overhead.
- Confidence: medium
- Source: Stanford SIEPR — https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/policy-brief/what-really-happening-jobs-separating-ai-hype-reality

### 4) Clinical failure rates have been ~91% for decades — and why that changes how you think about high-risk R&D
- Track: better_decisions
- Why it matters to me: Derek Lowe's synthesis of clinical failure rates (90% in the 1960s, 81% in the 70s, 80% in the 80s, 88% in the 90s, 91% in the 2000s, 91% in the 2010s) reveals a structural truth about high-stakes R&D: failure rates don't meaningfully improve even as techniques get more sophisticated. The brief dip in the 70s-80s was because targets were easier, not because science got better. Now that easy targets are exhausted, failure rates are back up.
- Tradeoff / tension: In a 91% failure environment, you can't optimize for individual project success — you optimize for portfolio strategy and learning rate. The industry's response (squeeze every dollar from successes) creates perverse incentives: risk aversion, bureaucratic inertia, and focus on easy-to-price markets rather than hardest-to-solve problems. This is a mirror for any founder in a high-failure domain.
- Implication: If you're building in a high-failure-rate space (which most startups are), the right framework isn't "how do I increase my odds of success?" but "how do I structure my approach to learn fast, fail cheap, and keep options open?" The pharma industry's mistake — continuing to test marginal candidates rather than killing them early — is a failure mode many founders replicate.
- Action: Audit your current projects using the pharma lens: are you continuing to invest in marginal candidates because of budget ownership dynamics rather than genuine promise? Build kill criteria that are independent of sunk cost.
- Confidence: high
- Source: Science.org (Derek Lowe) — https://www.science.org/content/blog-post/clinical-failure-rates-over-decades-yikes
