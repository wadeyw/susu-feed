### 1) Stripe: SaaS platforms are surging *despite* the SaaSpocalypse — the defensibility test is "what breaks when the customer turns it off"

- Track: own_business
- Why it matters to me: Stripe's payment-volume data (not equity prices) contradicts the "SaaSpocalypse" sell-off that wiped ~$1T from software stocks in 30 days. New platform businesses on Stripe are up 182% YoY over the last three months, and January 2026 platform cohorts are reaching $1M in payment volume faster than any prior cohort. But the durable part isn't the number — it's a reusable decision heuristic from Eric Noeth (Advent PE): "A high-signal indicator of platform defensibility is what breaks the day the customer turns it off." The strongest platforms share three traits: (1) they run key workflows (scheduling, inventory), (2) they retain valuable business information (customer history, pricing logic), and (3) they connect day-to-day operations to money movement. The more central to operations, the harder to dislodge. This is the same structural insight as the Voss "problem-finding is the whole job" thread from the last two days, now with a concrete, testable test.
- Tradeoff / tension: The test cuts both ways, and that's its value. It tells you when a wedge is defensible (the business *stops* when it's off) and when it's exposed (operations keep running). But the trap: the "retain data + connect to money" path requires owning the data and being in the money flow, which is slower to start and harder to reach cash flow than a pure feature/wedge. The "commodity build" advantage AI gives (cheap to build) only gets you to the door — it doesn't make the *embedding* cheaper, which is where the moat actually is. And the incentive skew is obvious: Stripe sells the platform infra, and the 182% is their own LLM + heuristic classification, so treat it as directional, not audited.
- Implication: When choosing a business wedge, apply the "what breaks when off" test *before* you build: would the customer's core operation stop if they turned your thing off? If yes (claims don't pay, trades don't settle, orders don't process), you're in defensible territory. If no (a nice-to-have overlay on an existing system), you're competing on a commodity build AI just made cheaper for everyone — thin moat. The winning move is vertical: embed into an industry whose data and money flow you can own, where "deep industry knowledge is tough to replicate."
- Action: Think (15 min) — take the two or three wedges you've been circling and score each on the three traits (runs workflows / retains data / connects to money) + the "what breaks when off" test. Write the scores into the knowledge base as a "platform defensibility" checklist. Discard wedges that fail "what breaks when off" — that's the cheapest filter you have.
- Confidence: medium-high (Stripe's own cross-network payment data, methodically described; caveats: their own classification + incentive skew, directional not audited; the Noeth heuristic is the durable, quotable part)

### 2) "How To Write With An LLM": the intellectual-PPE rule — never use a word the LLM suggests

- Track: daughters_future (serves better_decisions too)
- Why it matters to me: Thomas Ptacek (via Willison) makes a sharp, reusable rule for using LLMs on writing: **you may not use a single word an LLM suggests to you.** LLMs become copyeditors, fact-checkers, and proofreaders — not writing assistants. Willison endorses it as "intellectual personal protective equipment": adopt the strict rule that any specific turn of phrase an LLM offers is off-limits, and *stay strict about it.* That's a concrete boundary discipline, and it's exactly the skill your daughters will need most in an AI-native world: producing *their own* language — the one thing that doesn't transfer to the machine and is the durable one.
- Tradeoff / tension: The rule maximizes originality and discipline at a real cost: it's slower, and sometimes you *want* the LLM's phrasing because it's genuinely better than what you'd write, and you're forbidding yourself from taking the best available expression. The tension is speed/flow (let the LLM write, move fast) vs. ownership/judgment (keep the words yours, build the muscle). Ptacek's call is that ceding the words is silently ceding the thinking — the text "has that weird smell to it." For a founder and for kids alike: the skill you're protecting is the *self-expression* skill, which compounds and can't be delegated. The rule is a training device, not a productivity tool.
- Implication: This is a "how to use AI without letting it do the thinking" model, reusable for (a) your own writing (blog, docs, the knowledge base), and (b) the daughters' writing practice. The principle generalizes beyond writing: **the boundary belongs at the layer where judgment lives.** Let the machine own the mechanical layer (grammar, fact-check, search, formatting); keep the judgment layer (what to say, how to frame it, the word that's *yours*) yours. That's the same "layer under where you're working" habit from the influences/frameworks thread, applied to human–machine work division.
- Action: Act — adopt the rule for your own knowledge-base and writing work this week: when you use an LLM on text, never adopt a suggested phrase verbatim; rewrite it in your words and use the LLM only for the mechanical checks. For the daughters, make it a weekend habit: they write a short piece on something they care about, you read it and *don't* ask an LLM to smooth it — you ask them to defend their word choices. That's the verbalization/confidence muscle the rule is protecting.
- Confidence: high (Ptacek's rule is explicit and Willison endorses it with his own usage; the "judgment lives at the layer" generalization is my synthesis but is directly supported by the article's mechanical-vs-judgment structure)
- Source: Simon Willison quoting Thomas Ptacek — https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/17/how-to-write-with-an-llm/
