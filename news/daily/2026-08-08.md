- Action: Review your own agent deployments for similar escalation paths. Check for: unauthenticated endpoints, over-permissioned service accounts, IMDS exposure, legacy endpoints with known vulnerabilities. Assume agents will discover and chain weaknesses faster than your security team.

### 3) Water system controllers shouldn't be on the internet (but they are)
- Track: ai_change, better_decisions
- Why it matters to me: At least 12 US states' water systems have been hacked, likely by Iran-linked actors targeting programmable logic controllers (PLCs). The attack surface is massive: 50,000 municipal water systems, 90% of US water supply, many with no dedicated cybersecurity staff. This isn't theoretical—PLCs can turn pumps on and off, and the same patterns of discovery and exploitation we saw in the OpenAI incident apply here: agents will find the weak links.
- Tradeoff / tension: Critical infrastructure needs monitoring and remote management (which requires connectivity) versus air-gapped security (which limits operational capability). The tradeoff favors connectivity in practice, but the security posture hasn't caught up.
- Implication: AI agents will discover and exploit OT (operational technology) vulnerabilities faster than human attackers. The same privilege escalation patterns—finding weak credentials, chaining vulnerabilities, lateral movement—apply to industrial control systems. This isn't just a cybersecurity issue; it's a systemic risk that compounds with AI capability growth.
- Action: If you work with or near critical infrastructure, understand the exposure. The DEF CON Franklin project and Project Chimera are emerging responses, but the fundamental problem—thousands of undersourced municipalities running internet-connected control systems—remains unsolved.
